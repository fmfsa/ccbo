\begin{abstract}
Choosing effective interventions in complex systems often requires costly experiments. Causal Bayesian optimization guides these experiments using a causal graph, but errors in that graph can lead to poor predictions or exclude the best interventions. In practice, relationships between groups of variables may be more reliable than the detailed links between individual variables. Here we introduce causal Bayesian optimizers that use this coarse knowledge and intervene jointly on variables within selected groups. Their decisions are insensitive to graph errors that leave the relationships between groups unchanged. However, requiring joint interventions can also limit the outcomes they can achieve. We characterize this loss and identify conditions under which grouping variables preserves the best achievable outcome. Controlled experiments across three optimizer families examine these benefits and limitations, separating action restrictions from finite-budget differences due to modeling and optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many scientific problems involve intervening upon a complex system. Analytical representations of such systems are typically unavailable, complicating the process of selecting an optimal intervention. Bayesian optimization (BO) circumvents this issue by fitting a probabilistic surrogate model through sequential experimentation. However, BO methods treat the underlying system as a black box, and therefore do not exploit the experimenter's causal knowledge about the system to efficiently experiment.

Causal Bayesian optimization (CBO) \citep{agliettiCausalBayesianOptimization2020b} closes this gap by using a causal graph that captures underlying system dynamics to eliminate redundant intervention sets and improve estimates of multiple interventional effects at the same time via observational data. Existing CBO methods use directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) or acyclic directed mixed graphs (ADMGs) depending on the need to represent latent confounding. These graph-based reductions and causal priors rely on the supplied graph being correctly specified. In practice, graphs elicited from experts or estimated from data may be misspecified. A CBO method operating with a misspecified DAG may require significantly more experiments to identify the optimal intervention, or even worse, may preclude the optimal intervention from consideration entirely.

  $X_1\to Y$ changes the fine exploration set but not the quotient because the cluster edge $C_1\to Y$ remains through $X_2\to Y$. Orange nodes are manipulable, blue is the target, and the dash-dotted edge denotes latent
  confounding.}
  \label{fig:motivating}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} illustrates this failure mode. The confounded parents $X_1$ and $X_2$ jointly determine $Y$, and the optimum requires setting both. If the assumed variable-level graph (the \emph{fine graph}) omits $X_1\to Y$, CBO discards every intervention containing $X_1$. 

This observation motivates changing the resolution of the graph supplied to the optimizer. A partition $\Pi$ groups the variables into disjoint clusters. The resulting cluster-level directed acyclic graph (C-DAG) states relations between clusters while leaving their internal structure unspecified. In Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} (c) $X_1$ and $X_2$ form the cluster $C_1=\{X_1,X_2\}$. The edge $C_1\to Y$ survives through $X_2\to Y$, so the whole-cluster intervention remains available.

We call the resulting method quotient causal Bayesian optimization (QCBO). QCBO receives a partition and its C-DAG and intervenes on nonempty unions of manipulable clusters. Each cluster is represented by the vector of its member variables; intervening on it fixes every member. A cluster may encode a known subsystem, policy module, or other domain-defined unit whose external relations are more reliable than its internal structure. We assume that the practitioner provides the partition and C-DAG. Coarsening removes sensitivity to hidden within-cluster distinctions, but it can also remove useful fine-grained actions. It does not protect against errors that change the C-DAG: deleting both parent edges in Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} removes $C_1\to Y$ and therefore changes the C-DAG consumed by QCBO.

We make three contributions: 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce cluster-based versions of causal Bayesian optimizers that use a supplied cluster graph and intervene jointly within selected clusters.
    \item We characterize their robustness to graph errors that leave the cluster graph unchanged, the loss from restricting interventions, and conditions under which that loss is zero.
    \item We use controlled experiments to separate errors that remove useful interventions from errors that alter causal priors, and evaluate the construction across optimizer families.
\end{enumerate}

 Proofs, complete dataset graphs, and additional results are provided in the appendices.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Causal models and interventions}

A structural causal model (SCM) \(\mathcal M=\langle\mathbf U,\mathbf V,F,P(\mathbf U)\rangle\) consists of exogenous variables \(\mathbf U\), endogenous variables \(\mathbf V\), their structural mechanisms \(F=\{f_V:V\in\mathbf V\}\), and an exogenous distribution \(P(\mathbf U)\) \citep{pearlCausality2009}. Each endogenous variable is assigned by 
 \[
 V:=f_V\!\left(\operatorname{Pa}(V),\mathbf U_V\right),
\]

where \(\operatorname{Pa}(V)\) are its parents and \(\mathbf U_V\subseteq\mathbf U\) are its exogenous inputs. We write \(\operatorname{An}(V)\) for the ancestors of \(V\).

Let \(Y\in\mathbf V\) be the target and $\mathbf X\subseteq\mathbf V\setminus\{Y\}$ the manipulable variables. An intervention consists of a nonempty set \(X\subseteq\mathbf X\) and assigned values \(x\in D(X)\), where \(D(X)\) is the intervention domain. We use fixed nonempty per-variable domains \(D_v\) and set \(D(X)=\prod_{v\in X}D_v\), so restrictions of admissible assignments remain admissible. This does not require the domains to contain all natural response values; that stronger condition is stated separately in Definition~\ref{def:full-support}. The operator \(\doo(X=x)\) replaces the structural mechanisms of the variables in \(X\) by the constants \(x\). The resulting interventional response is
\begin{equation}
f_X(x):=\E_{\mathcal M}[Y\mid\doo(X=x)].
\label{eq:arm-value}
\end{equation}

A DAG represents the directed relations among the endogenous variables when all relevant common causes are observed. When latent common causes are present, the latent projection of the SCM is an ADMG \(\Gg\): directed edges represent causal relations and bidirected edges represent latent confounding. Throughout, \(\Gg\) denotes the graph of the true data-generating SCM and \(\widehat{\Gg}\) denotes the graph supplied to the optimizer. Changing \(\widehat{\Gg}\) can change the optimizer's decisions, but it does not change the outcomes generated by \(\mathcal M\).

\subsection{Causal Bayesian optimization}

Causal Bayesian optimization (\CBO{}) \citep{agliettiCausalBayesianOptimization2020b} searches for an intervention that minimizes Equation~\eqref{eq:arm-value}. It maintains a Gaussian process (GP) for each retained intervention set $X$, also called an \emph{arm}. Its exploration set consists of the graph's minimal intervention sets (MIS) \citep{lee2018structural,agliettiCausalBayesianOptimization2020b}. Writing \(\widehat{\Gg}_{\bar X}\) for the graph obtained by deleting edges with an arrowhead into any variable in \(X\), this set is

\begin{equation}
\ES(\widehat{\Gg})=\operatorname{MIS}(\widehat{\Gg})
=\{\emptyset\ne X\subseteq\mathbf X:
X\subseteq\An_{\widehat{\Gg}_{\bar X}}(Y)\}.
\label{eq:mis}
\end{equation}

Thus \(X\) is retained only when every intervened variable remains an ancestor of \(Y\) after the intervention removes incoming edges to \(X\).

For every retained arm, CBO places a GP prior on its response surface,

\begin{equation}
f_X\sim\mathcal{GP}(m_X^{\widehat{\Gg}},k_X^{\widehat{\Gg}}).
\label{eq:cbo-gp}
\end{equation}

where \(m_X^{\widehat{\Gg}}\) and \(k_X^{\widehat{\Gg}}\) are the mean and covariance functions. When the effect is identified under \(\widehat{\Gg}\), the prior mean uses an observational estimate,
\begin{equation}
  m_X^{\widehat{\mathcal G}}(x)
  :=
  \widehat{\E}_{\widehat{\mathcal G}}\!\left[Y\mid\doo(X=x)\right].
  \label{eq:causal-prior}
\end{equation}

and the covariance can be defined as:
\begin{equation}
k_X^{\widehat{\Gg}}(x,x')
=
k_{\mathrm{RBF}}(x,x')
+
\sigma_X(x)\sigma_X(x'),
\label{eq:causal-kernel}
\end{equation}
where
\[
\sigma_X(x)
:=
\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}
       [Y\mid\doo(X=x)]},
\]
\[
k_{\mathrm{RBF}}(x,x')
:=
\sigma_f^2
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\right).
\]



The causal estimate predicts the outcome of an intervention before that intervention has been tried, using observational data and the identifying assumptions encoded by the supplied graph. Our arm-based implementation uses this estimate as the GP mean and also uses an estimated interventional predictive variance in the covariance, fully detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:estimators}. When identification fails we use a constant observational fallback: the observational mean and variance of \(Y\). Each arm's GP is conditioned on its interventional data. On an intervention trial, the optimizer selects the arm and values maximizing expected improvement per unit intervention cost.


The supplied graph therefore affects CBO through two objects:

\[
\widehat{\Gg}\longmapsto
 \left(\ES(\widehat{\Gg}),
 \{m_X^{\widehat{\Gg}},k_X^{\widehat{\Gg}}\}_{X\in\ES(\widehat{\Gg})}\right).
\]

A graph error can change the prior information, the available arms, or both.

\subsection{Dynamic and model-based CBO}

Dynamic CBO (DCBO) \citep{agliettiDynamicCausalBayesian2021a} extends CBO to temporal causal models. It selects interventions in successive time slices, using the slice graphs and temporal steps to transfer information over time.

Model-based CBO (MCBO) \citep{sussexMODELBASEDCAUSALBAYESIAN2023} instead places GP models on the individual structural mechanisms and composes them via the causal graph. It then optimizes interventions using an optimistic acquisition rule that propagates mechanism-level uncertainty through the graph. 

These methods depend on the supplied graph in distinct ways: DCBO through its temporal graph components and MCBO through its mechanism factorization.

\subsection{Cluster causal graphs}
Let \(\Pi\) be a partition of \(\mathbf V\), and let \(\chi:\mathbf V\to\Pi\) map each variable to its unique cluster. The quotient graph \(\Gg^\Pi\) has a directed edge \(\chi(u)\to\chi(v)\) whenever \(u\to v\) is present in \(\Gg\) and \(\chi(u)\ne\chi(v)\); bidirected edges are mapped analogously. The quotient is a cluster-level causal graph, or C-DAG. A partition is \emph{valid} when the directed part of its quotient is acyclic \citep{madaleno2026a, madalenoCoarseningLinearNonGaussian2026}.

A C-DAG preserves which causal and confounding relations exist between clusters while leaving within-cluster structure unspecified. C-DAG identification is sound and complete over the fine graphs represented by the quotient \citep{CausalEffectIdentification}.

The idea of considering coarsened graphical models was originally proposed by \citet{parviainen2017learning} under the name of variable groups and then formalized as C-DAGs by \citet{CausalEffectIdentification}; since then, C-DAGs have been object of interest both for causal inference \citep{ferreira2025identifying} and causal discovery \citep{ninad2025causal,wahl2024foundations,anand2026causal}.

\section{Quotient causal optimizers}
\label{sec:methods}
In this section, we introduce quotient counterparts of three CBO methods. The goal is to make their decisions robust to fine-graph errors that leave a chosen quotient unchanged, at the cost of restricting interventions to unions of clusters. We first present the arm-based QCBO construction, then extend the same principle to dynamic and model-based CBO, and finally introduce an adaptive refinement scheme that can recover finer-grained actions. Within each declared comparison, the fine and quotient methods share the specified acquisition and update policy; common numerical corrections and the coherent dynamic conditional model are described in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

\subsection{Arm-based QCBO}

Quotient CBO (QCBO) receives the partition \(\Pi\), supplied C-DAG \(\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi}\), target \(Y\), observational data, and intervention domains. In our implementation, only manipulable variables are grouped; all nonmanipulable variables, including the target, remain singletons. Thus every cluster is either fully manipulable or not intervened on. Grouping purely nonmanipulable variables would leave the intervention family unchanged, but could change the quotient's identifying information; we do not implement that extension here. Let

Let $\Pi_{\mathbf X}:=\{C\in\Pi:C\subseteq\mathbf X\}$ be the
manipulable clusters and $C_Y:=\chi(Y)$ the target cluster.
QCBO applies the MIS rule to the C-DAG and expands each retained
set of clusters to its member variables:
\begin{equation}
\ESpi(\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi})
:=\left\{\bigcup_{C\in S}C:
\substack{\emptyset\ne S\subseteq\Pi_{\mathbf X},\\
S\subseteq\An(C_Y)\text{ in }\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi}_{\bar S}}
\right\}.
\label{eq:qcbo-arms}
\end{equation}


Every retained arm intervenes on complete clusters. We apply
causal-effect identification to the supplied C-DAG and construct
each arm's GP prior as in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. We assume a
valid partition and a sound, complete identification procedure.
Our numerical estimators support backdoor adjustment, frontdoor
adjustment, and $g$-computation. If an effect is not identified,
or its identified functional is unsupported, the arm uses the
observational mean and variance of $Y$ as its fallback prior
(Appendix~\ref{app:estimators}).

The singleton partition recovers the fine graph and the nonempty-MIS exploration set. Its prior construction agrees arm by arm with matched CBO when the identification, estimation and numerical routines coincide. A formal statement distinguishing construction identity from numerical equivalence appears in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Dynamic and model-based quotient variants}

Quotient Dynamic CBO (QDCBO) replaces every time-slice and its temporal transitions by their quotients and selects cluster-union interventions. Our coherent adaptation of DCBO \citep{agliettiDynamicCausalBayesian2021a} fits each mechanism conditional on the union of contemporaneous and lagged external parents. Population responses integrate the complete intervention history: earlier selected manipulator actions remain fixed, while free past variables, including outcomes, are re-simulated.

Quotient model-based CBO (\QMCBO{}) replaces MCBO's \citep{sussexMODELBASEDCAUSALBAYESIAN2023} node mechanisms by structural cluster kernels
\[
K_C(\mathrm d\mathbf v_C\mid\mathbf v_{\operatorname{pa}(C)}),
\]
and composes them along the C-DAG. Here $\mathbf v_C$ collects the variables in cluster $C$, and $\operatorname{pa}(C)$ is its set of parent clusters. Each kernel is induced by composing the member-level SCM mechanisms at the specified parent values. Under causal sufficiency and a valid partition it agrees with the observational conditional almost surely in the observational parent distribution; observational data need not determine its extension to every parent value. Joint mechanisms preserve within-cluster dependence, while their fitted GP approximations need not recover the structural kernels.

In the corrected comparison, fine nodes and singleton clusters share the same fixed-noise GP, supplied additive-noise variance, scalar output standardization and marginal-likelihood fit. Multi-variable clusters use a Kronecker multi-task GP with full-rank task-noise likelihood; this joint residual noise is included once. Both methods share the corrected intervention map and input normalization. Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} distinguishes full-menu fine MCBO from its matched-menu control.

Under causal sufficiency, mutually independent node disturbances and a valid partition, the structural cluster factorization is sound for whole-cluster interventions. It does not determine effects of interventions on strict subsets of a cluster; two fine models can agree on every quotient-level input and disagree on such a sub-cluster effect. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} states and proves both facts.

\subsection{Adaptive refinement}
Hierarchical QCBO (\HQCBO{}) starts from a coarse partition and a
supplied hierarchy of allowable cluster splits. The hierarchy
specifies the members of each child cluster. When the plateau rule
fires, HQCBO splits the refinable clusters in the incumbent arm.
For each split cluster, it adds every previously unavailable
nonempty union of its child clusters as an arm with a plain GP prior.
Existing arms retain their interventional data and use causal priors
derived from the initial quotient. The initial quotient remains the
only graph used throughout the run.

Refinement is triggered when the incumbent objective improves by less than \(10^{-3}\) over five trials. In the corrected noisy protocol, this incumbent uses measured responses. Each new arm requires three paid initial measurements before sequential selection; the MediatedChain split adds two singleton arms and costs six units. A split is refused if its complete design is unaffordable. The hierarchy and trigger are heuristic; Section~\ref{sec:refinement} states conditional recovery and invariance guarantees, not finite-budget success.

\section{Robustness and the price of coarsening}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Quotient invariance}
\label{sec:invariance}

Fix a valid partition $\Pi$. Let
$\xi=(\mathcal M,\mathcal D_0,\omega,\eta)$ collect the true SCM,
initial data, all optimizer and environment randomness, and fixed
settings, including intervention domains and costs. Write
$\tau_{\mathcal A}(\widehat{\Gg},\xi)$ for the complete trajectory:
selected arms, intervention values, observations, and incumbents.

An optimizer satisfies the \emph{quotient contract} if every
graph-dependent operation uses only $\Pi$ and
$\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi}$. For the true graph $\Gg$, define
\begin{equation}
E_\Pi(\Gg):=
\{\widehat{\Gg}\text{ ADMG over }\mathbf V:
\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi}=\Gg^{\Pi}\}.
\label{eq:protected-class}
\end{equation}
This class includes within-cluster edits and cross-cluster edits
that leave every quotient edge unchanged.

\begin{proposition}[Quotient invariance]
\label{prop:contract}
At fixed $\Pi$ and $\xi$, an optimizer satisfying the quotient
contract obeys
\[
\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi}=(\widehat{\Gg}')^{\Pi}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tau_{\mathcal A}(\widehat{\Gg},\xi)
=\tau_{\mathcal A}(\widehat{\Gg}',\xi).
\]
\end{proposition}

Equal inputs give equal decisions and, under shared environment
randomness, equal observations; induction gives identical runs.
QCBO and QMCBO satisfy this contract under the declared implementations; for QDCBO it must cover both within-slice and cross-slice inputs. At the singleton partition, the graph and action construction recover their fine counterparts; numerical equivalence additionally requires the matching implementation conditions in Proposition~\ref{prop:identity}.

\subsection{Price of restricting interventions}
\label{sec:price}

The action family allowed by \(\Pi\) consists of nonempty unions of manipulable clusters:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal U(\Pi):=
 \left\{\bigcup_{C\in S}C:
 \emptyset\ne S\subseteq\Pi_{\mathbf X}\right\}.
 \label{eq:cluster-actions}
\end{equation}
We assume that the structural mechanisms are measurable, the target
is integrable under every admissible intervention, and the population
minima below are attained. For an intervention set \(X\), define its
best population value by
\[
 \mu^\star(X):=\min_{x\in D(X)}f_X(x).
\]
Let \(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}}:=\{\{v\}:v\in\mathbf V\}\) denote the singleton partition. The best value reachable at resolution \(\Pi\) and its loss relative to the fine action space are


\begin{equation}
 V(\Pi):=\min_{X\in\mathcal U(\Pi)}\mu^\star(X),
 \qquad
 \Delta(\Pi):=V(\Pi)-V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}}).
 \label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
We call \(\Delta(\Pi)\ge0\) the \emph{price of coarsening}.
 
A partition \(\Pi'\) \emph{refines} \(\Pi\), written \(\Pi'\preceq\Pi\), when every cluster of \(\Pi'\) is contained in a cluster of \(\Pi\). Refinement enlarges the set of representable interventions.

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity and price of coarsening]
\label{prop:price}
For valid $\Pi'\preceq\Pi$,
\[
 \mathcal U(\Pi)\subseteq\mathcal U(\Pi'),
 \qquad
 0\le\Delta(\Pi')\le\Delta(\Pi).
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \Delta(\Pi)=0
 \iff
 \arg\min_{X\in\mathcal U(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})}\mu^\star(X)
 \cap\mathcal U(\Pi)\ne\emptyset.
\]
\end{proposition}

Thus coarsening is lossless exactly when at least one fine-optimal intervention is a union of clusters. The MIS-reduction result in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} preserves the optimum when the null intervention is included and the graph is compatible with the evaluated SCM. For the nonempty families used here, equality with the retained quotient-MIS value needs an additional condition, such as a retained action with population value at most \(\E_{\mathcal M}[Y]\). Proposition~\ref{prop:price} concerns \(\mathcal U(\Pi)\) directly and does not require this equality. Even when \(\Delta(\Pi)=0\), a partition can change arm count, intervention dimension, cost, and finite-budget behavior.

\subsection{Protected-class regret separation}
\label{sec:separation}


Let \(\hat z_t=(\hat X_t,\hat x_t)\) be an incumbent selected among executed interventions using the learner's available information after trial \(t\), and define its population value by
\[
 \mu(\hat z_t):=\E_{\mathcal M}
 [Y\mid\doo(\hat X_t=\hat x_t)].
 \]
With \(y^\star:=V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})\), define instantaneous and cumulative incumbent regret by
\begin{equation}
 r_t:=\mu(\hat z_t)-y^\star,
 \qquad
 R_T:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t.
 \label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
This metric sums the gaps of the incumbent recommendations, starting after an eligible intervention has been executed; observation-only initialization creates no incumbent. It does not require their population values to decrease. Under noisy feedback, a minimum-measured-response recommendation can worsen in population value; independent population scoring evaluates that recommendation and does not select it. Equation~\eqref{eq:regret-decomposition} remains an algebraic decomposition for each eligible cluster-union recommendation.

Fix a true SCM \(\mathcal M\) with graph \(\Gg\), while the optimizer receives a hypothesis graph \(\widehat{\Gg}\). Let \(R_T(\mathcal A,\widehat{\Gg})\) denote regret at fixed \(\xi\), and define worst-case regret over the protected class by
\[
 R_T^{\mathrm{wc}}(\mathcal A,\Pi,\Gg)
 :=\sup_{\widehat{\Gg}\in E_\Pi(\Gg)}
 R_T(\mathcal A,\widehat{\Gg}).
 \]
For a cluster-union incumbent,
\begin{equation}
 \mu(\hat z_t)-y^\star
 =\underbrace{V(\Pi)-y^\star}_{\text{coarsening price}}
 +\underbrace{\mu(\hat z_t)-V(\Pi)}_{\text{optimization error}}.
\label{eq:regret-decomposition}
\end{equation}
 
A \emph{semi-Markovian SCM} has mutually independent exogenous variables, each of which is a parent of at most two endogenous variables; shared exogenous parents are represented by the bidirected edges of its latent-projection ADMG.

\begin{theorem}[Protected-class regret separation]
\label{thm:separation}
The following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
 \item For every \(\beta>0\), there exist a semi-Markovian SCM, a valid
 partition with \(\Delta(\Pi)=0\), and a hypothesis
 \(\widehat{\Gg}\in E_\Pi(\Gg)\) obtained by deleting one directed edge, such
 that every optimizer whose initial and sequential interventions lie in
 \(\operatorname{MIS}(\widehat{\Gg})\) and whose incumbent is selected among
 executed interventions satisfies
 \[
  r_t\ge\beta\quad\text{for every }t,
  \qquad
  R_T^{\mathrm{wc}}(\mathcal A,\Pi,\Gg)\ge\beta T.
 \]
\item If an optimizer satisfies Proposition~\ref{prop:contract}, executes only cluster-union interventions, and selects its incumbent among executed actions, then its trajectory is identical for every \(\widehat{\Gg}\in E_\Pi(\Gg)\) and
 \[
 \begin{aligned}
 R_T^{\mathrm{wc}}(\mathcal A,\Pi,\Gg)
 &=R_T(\mathcal A,\Gg)\\
 &=T\Delta(\Pi)
 +\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\mu(\hat z_t)-V(\Pi)\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
 \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Part (i) is instantiated by the exploration-set corruption experiment in Section~\ref{sec:exp-a}: a quotient-invisible edge deletion can force linear regret even when coarsening is lossless. Part (ii) separates the unavoidable structural price from optimization error within the quotient action space.

\subsection{When coarsening is lossless}
\label{sec:zero-price}

For a nonempty intervention set \(X\subseteq\mathbf X\), define its
\emph{cluster completion}
\[
X^\Pi:=\bigcup\{C\in\Pi:C\cap X\ne\emptyset\}.
\]
This is the smallest union of clusters containing \(X\).
Completing an intervention on \(X\) also fixes the variables
\(B:=X^\Pi\setminus X\), which would otherwise respond naturally.

\begin{definition}[Full intervention support]
\label{def:full-support}
A partition has full intervention support if, for every nonempty
\(X\subseteq\mathbf X\) and \(x\in D(X)\), every value in the support
of the natural response of \(B=X^\Pi\setminus X\) under
\(\doo(X=x)\) is an admissible completion: \((x,b)\in D(X^\Pi)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Exogenous separation under cluster completion]
\label{def:ccc}
In a semi-Markovian SCM, a partition satisfies exogenous separation
under cluster completion if, for every nonempty
\(X\subseteq\mathbf X\), the following sets are disjoint:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item the exogenous ancestors of \(X^\Pi\setminus X\) after
intervening on \(X\);
\item the exogenous ancestors of \(Y\) after intervening on
\(X^\Pi\).
\end{enumerate}
Ancestors are evaluated in the underlying SCM graph after replacing
the intervened variables' equations by constants.
\end{definition}

Full support ensures that the natural values of the added variables
can also be assigned by intervention. Exogenous separation ensures
that the disturbances generating those values are independent of the
disturbances that still affect \(Y\) after completion. The fine
intervention's expected outcome is then an average of the outcomes
of admissible completed interventions, so some completion performs
at least as well.

\begin{corollary}[Structural zero-price condition]
\label{cor:zero-price}
In a semi-Markovian SCM, full intervention support and exogenous
separation under cluster completion imply
\[
V(\Pi)=V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Delta(\Pi)=0.
\]
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Example: lossless completion despite internal confounding.}
Let
\[
X_1=U+\varepsilon_1,\qquad
X_2=U+\varepsilon_2,\qquad
Y=X_1^2+X_2^2+\varepsilon_Y,
\]
where \(U,\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_Y\) are mutually
independent and uniform on \([-1/2,1/2]\).
Group \(X_1,X_2\) into one manipulable cluster, with intervention
domain \([-1,1]^2\). Under \(\doo(X_1=x_1)\), the added variable
\(X_2\) depends on \(U,\varepsilon_2\); after fixing both variables,
\(Y\) depends only on \(\varepsilon_Y\). These exogenous sets are
disjoint, even though \(U\) confounds \(X_1\) and \(X_2\).
Moreover,
\[
f_{\{X_1\}}(x_1)=x_1^2+\tfrac16,
\qquad
f_{\{X_1,X_2\}}(x_1,0)=x_1^2.
\]
The same argument applies with the variables exchanged.
Both resolutions attain value \(0\), so coarsening is lossless.

These conditions are sufficient but not necessary.
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} proves the corollary and gives an example
showing why exogenous separation matters.

\subsection{Fixed partitions and refinement}
\label{sec:refinement}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regret-decomposition} gives $r_t\ge\Delta(\Pi)$ and $R_T\ge T\Delta(\Pi)$ for incumbents restricted to $\mathcal U(\Pi)$. A positive coarsening price therefore creates a floor that learning within the fixed action space cannot remove. Refinement can remove this floor by exposing
a fine-optimal intervention.

\begin{definition}[Incumbent consistency]
\label{def:consistency}
An optimizer is incumbent-consistent on an arm family \(\mathcal F\)
if continued optimization over that family satisfies
\[
\mu(\hat z_t)\longrightarrow
\min_{X\in\mathcal F}\mu^\star(X)
\]
almost surely.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Conditional refinement recovery]
\label{cor:recovery}
Suppose that, after finitely many trials, HQCBO has exposed a fixed
arm family \(\mathcal F\) containing a fine-optimal intervention and
thereafter optimizes over that family. If the optimizer is
incumbent-consistent on \(\mathcal F\), then, almost surely, \(r_t\to0\) and \(R_T/T\to0\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Adaptive refinement invariance]
\label{cor:adaptive-invariance}
Fix the hierarchy $\mathcal H$, the initial partition $\Pi_0$, and all other inputs and randomness. Suppose that every graph-dependent operation, including operations after refinement, uses only the initial quotient. Let $\tau_{\mathcal H}$ denote the resulting trajectory. Then
\[
 \Gg^{\Pi_0}=(\Gg')^{\Pi_0}
 \Longrightarrow
 \tau_{\mathcal H}(\Gg)=\tau_{\mathcal H}(\Gg'),
\]
including every split and every trial after it.
\end{corollary}

Recovery is conditional on refinement exposing a fine-optimal
intervention and on subsequent incumbent consistency. We do not establish that consistency for the minimum-measured-response rule under noisy feedback; exposing an optimal arm alone does not imply that the implemented recommendation attains its optimum.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:exp-design}

We use \textsc{ParallelParent}, \textsc{FrontDoor}, and \textsc{MediatedChain} to isolate arm loss, prior corruption, and the price of coarsening, respectively. Their closed-form interventional objectives let us score recommendations exactly, separately from the noisy measurements available to the learner. We also compare fine and quotient optimizers on dynamic and model-based benchmarks. Graphs and partitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:dags}; protocols are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

Every misspecification comparison is paired: the true SCM, observational data, initial measurements on common arms, and separately keyed random streams are shared; only the graph supplied to the optimizer changes. The corrected controlled suite reports the population gap of the measured-data recommendation at a fixed total intervention budget and its sum at integer cost checkpoints. Initialization and refinement purchases count toward this budget. These cost-indexed summaries are distinct from the trial-indexed regret in Equation~\eqref{eq:regret}. Protected-pair audits compare complete action, measurement, recommendation and cost traces, rather than only final values.

Controls isolate what each comparison measures. \BO{} optimizes the joint intervention set with no graph. \BOS{} treats every nonempty subset as an arm with plain GP priors. \CBONP{} keeps the graph-derived CBO arms but drops the causal prior. The FrontDoor CBO-FALLBACK control keeps the correct graph and arms while replacing both prior mean and variance by the same observational fallback used under misspecification. Dynamic and model-based comparisons include fine/full-menu, fine/coarse-menu and quotient/coarse-menu methods, separating menu restrictions from model construction.

\subsection{Exploration-set corruption}
\label{sec:exp-a}

The \textsc{ParallelParent} SCM is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} and Appendix~\ref{app:dags}. Its structural equations are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 U&=0.4\varepsilon_0,\\ X_1&=U+0.3\varepsilon_1,\\
 X_2&=U+0.3\varepsilon_2,\\
 Y&=(X_1-2)^2+(X_2+2)^2
  +0.1\varepsilon_Y.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:parallel-parent-scm}
\end{equation}
The disturbances are independent standard Gaussians, and $D(X_1)=D(X_2)=[-3,3]$.
Under the correct graph, the fine MIS are
$\{X_1\}$, $\{X_2\}$, and $\{X_1,X_2\}$, and the joint arm attains

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Graph-agnostic and causal entropy optimization methods learn over candidate graphs online \citep{mukherjeeGraphAgnosticCausal2024,branchiniCausalEntropyOptimization2023}, while causal-bandit guarantees price learning an unknown graph within the horizon \citep{luCausalBanditsUnknown2021}. Our setting instead studies commitment to a fixed coarse input. These approaches are complementary: a graph learner could operate over, or trigger refinement of, a quotient.

The construction builds on cluster-DAG identification \citep{CausalEffectIdentification} and is related to causal abstraction and macro-level structure learning \citep{beckersAbstractingCausalModels2019,madaleno2026a}. We coarsen manipulable inputs and explicitly characterize the actions removed by that choice. MCBO already permits vector-valued mechanisms \citep{sussexMODELBASEDCAUSALBAYESIAN2023}; our contribution is their use on a supplied quotient with whole-cluster interventions. Original CBO also discusses natural mediator responses outside intervention domains \citep{agliettiCausalBayesianOptimization2020b}; here the completion result gives sufficient conditions for losslessness.

In attempting to optimally solve a sequential decision-making problem, CBO is closely related to multi-armed bandit (MAB) algorithms. Causal MABs (CMABs), that is MAB problems where an agent intervenes on an SCM that generates reward, were first studied by \citet{bareinboim2015bandits} and \citet{lattimore2016causal}. Several works have studied different aspects of CMABs such as using graphical information to sub-select potentially optimal actions \citep{lee2018structural,lee2019structural}, optimizing under a budget \citep{nair2021budgeted}, learning with limited or missing graphical information \cite{park2025structural,konobeev2023causal}. A setting related to ours is causally abstracted MABs (CAMABs) \citep{zennaro2024causally,dyer2025using}, where decision problems are related by a causal abstraction \citep{rubenstein2017causal,schooltink2024aligning}. In particular, AT-UCB uses an abstract model to eliminate candidate actions and relates optimal rewards across levels. QCBO instead fixes a supplied quotient and studies invariance to quotient-preserving graph errors and the price of restricting interventions to whole clusters; abstraction-based action elimination is not claimed as new.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Exact invariance holds between supplied graphs with the same quotient. Protection relative to a true-graph run requires that shared quotient to match the true quotient. Invariance does not imply low regret: an optimizer can behave identically under protected graph errors while suffering from an uninformative prior or a restricted action space. The sufficient zero-price conditions are properties of the true SCM and intervention domains; QCBO does not require them to run. Exogenous separation is a structural assumption that cannot generally be verified from observational data alone. Refinement is heuristic, with recovery conditional on exposing a fine-optimal intervention and on subsequent incumbent consistency. Different partitions also induce different arm counts, initialization costs, and intervention costs. Finally, QMCBO inherits the causal-sufficiency
assumption of its model-based factorization.

The controlled examples were constructed after exploratory work and do not establish average benefit across real problems. The noisy minimum-measured-response rule is not proved incumbent-consistent, and its population quality need not improve monotonically. Dynamic residual covariance and finite-particle integration are approximations; the regularized nonstationary SCM is a separate benchmark. The committed-graph CEO comparator cannot represent the latent confounding in ParallelParent or FrontDoor. The PSA study is a synthetic simulator comparison, not clinical validation. Static and MCBO conclusions await their complete frozen-matrix audits.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Quotient causal optimizers satisfying the quotient contract are invariant to graph errors that leave the supplied C-DAG unchanged. Restricting interventions to unions of clusters can incur a price, which cannot increase under refinement and vanishes under the stated structural conditions. Hierarchical refinement offers a conditional route to recovering the fine optimum.

\section{Proofs and additional technical results}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Method-specific results}

\begin{proposition}[Singleton construction identity]
\label{prop:identity}
At \(\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}}\), the quotient graph equals the fine graph, the QCBO exploration set equals \(\operatorname{MIS}(\widehat{\Gg})\), and its prior construction coincides arm by arm with matched CBO when the identification and estimation routines agree. The dynamic quotient graphs and model-based structural factorization likewise reduce to their fine counterparts. Equality of optimizer trajectories additionally requires matching GP parameterization, acquisition, initialization, observation policy, numerical settings and random-number streams.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    At the singleton partition, \(\chi\) is one-to-one and quotienting changes no edge. Unions of manipulable singleton clusters are exactly the subsets of \(\mathbf X\), so Equation~\eqref{eq:qcbo-arms} is Equation~\eqref{eq:mis}. The C-DAG identification queries are then the same queries used by matched nonempty-MIS CBO; equal numerical estimators yield equal priors. The dynamic slice graphs and model-based joint cluster mechanisms also reduce to their node-level counterparts. These facts establish construction identity. When the additional implementation inputs and routines coincide, induction over updates gives equal trajectories.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Soundness of structural cluster kernels]
\label{prop:cluster-soundness}
Assume an acyclic causally sufficient SCM \(\mathcal M\) with mutually independent node disturbances and a valid partition. Let
$\emptyset\ne S\subseteq\Pi_{\mathbf X}$ and
$X=\bigcup_{C\in S}C$. For every $x\in D(X)$,
\[
 P_{\mathcal M}(d\mathbf v_{\mathbf V\setminus X}\mid\doo(X=x))
 =\left.
 \prod_{C\in\Pi\setminus S}
 K_C(d\mathbf v_C\mid\mathbf v_{\operatorname{pa}(C)})
 \right|_{X=x}.
\]
Here \(K_C\) is the SCM-defined cluster kernel, and the product composes these kernels in quotient topological order, with intervened coordinates fixed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each node equation and its independent disturbance define a structural kernel at every parent assignment. Compose the member kernels within each cluster in their internal topological order to obtain \(K_C\). A whole-cluster intervention deletes all member kernels of each intervened cluster and fixes its coordinates. Grouping the remaining kernels gives the displayed law; validity orders them along the C-DAG. Independence of cluster disturbances from parent clusters makes \(K_C\) a version of the observational conditional, equal almost surely in the observational parent distribution. The SCM defines its values elsewhere; observational data alone need not identify this extension, even at individual zero-probability points in the support.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Sub-cluster effects are not identified by cluster mechanisms]
\label{prop:qexposure}
There exist two causally sufficient SCMs with the same valid quotient, joint cluster mechanisms, and whole-cluster interventional distributions, but different values of \(P(Y\mid\doo(X=x))\) for a strict subset \(X\) of a cluster.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(C=\{X_2,X_3\}\) be a root cluster and \(Y=X_2+\varepsilon_Y\). In one SCM take \(X_2=\varepsilon_2\) and \(X_3=cX_2+\varepsilon_3\); in another, choose the reverse linear-Gaussian factorization of the same joint distribution. The models agree on the joint law of \(C\) and every whole-cluster intervention. Under \(\doo(X_3=x)\), however, \(X_2\) remains mean zero in the first model and changes linearly with \(x\) in the second. Quotient-level inputs therefore do not identify the sub-cluster effect.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Quotient invariance}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:contract}]
Write each decision as
\[
d_t=f_t\!\left(\Pi,\widehat{\Gg}^{\Pi},
\mathcal D_{t-1},\omega_t,\eta\right).
\]
Equal quotients, initial data, optimizer randomness, and fixed
settings give equal first decisions. Applying the same intervention
to the same true SCM with shared environment randomness gives
equal observations and updated datasets. Induction establishes
equality of every decision, observation, incumbent, and stopping
event.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Price and protected-class separation}

\begin{lemma}[MIS reduction with the null intervention]
\label{lem:mis-reduction}
Let \(\mathcal G\) be the latent-projection ADMG of the acyclic SCM \(\mathcal M\) whose interventional distributions are evaluated, with manipulable variables \(\mathbf X\) and target \(Y\). Assume the product-domain convention of Section~2.1, the population attainment assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:price}, and integrability of the target also under the null intervention. For \(X\subseteq\mathbf X\), define
\[
 X':=X\cap\An_{\mathcal G_{\bar X}}(Y).
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
 \item for every \(x\in D(X)\),
 \[
  P(Y\mid\doo(X=x))
  =P\!\left(Y\mid\doo(X'=x|_{X'})\right);
 \]
\item if \(X'\ne\varnothing\), then \(X'\in\operatorname{MIS}(\mathcal G)\); \item defining \(D(\varnothing)=\{\varnothing\}\) and \(f_{\varnothing}(\varnothing)=\mu_{\varnothing}:=\E_{\mathcal M}[Y]\), optimizing over \(\{\varnothing\}\cup\operatorname{MIS}(\mathcal G)\) attains the same population optimum as optimizing over all subsets of \(\mathbf X\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set \(R:=X\setminus X'\). Suppose that some \(r\in R\) has a directed path to \(Y\) in \(\mathcal G_{\bar X'}\). This path cannot enter \(X'\). Let \(r^\star\) be the last member of \(R\) on the path. The subpath from \(r^\star\) to \(Y\) contains no further member of \(X\), so it also exists in \(\mathcal G_{\bar X}\). This contradicts \(r^\star\notin\An_{\mathcal G_{\bar X}}(Y)\). Thus, while \(X'\) remains intervened on, variables in \(R\) cannot causally affect \(Y\), proving (i) by coupling the same exogenous disturbances. The product-domain convention makes the restricted assignment admissible.

For every \(v\in X'\), a directed path from \(v\) to \(Y\) in \(\mathcal G_{\bar X}\) cannot enter another member of \(X\). It therefore also exists in \(\mathcal G_{\bar X'}\), proving (ii). Applying (i) and (ii) to every candidate intervention proves (iii).
\end{proof}
 
For a valid quotient of this true graph, put \(\mathcal E_\Pi=\ESpi(\Gg^{\Pi})\) and \(W(\Pi)=\min_{X\in\mathcal E_\Pi}\mu^\star(X)\), with \(W(\Pi)=+\infty\) if \(\mathcal E_\Pi\) is empty. All intervened clusters are fully manipulable. The same last-removed-ancestor argument applies to whole clusters: after retained clusters are fixed, a fine directed path from a removed cluster to \(Y\) would project to a directed quotient path, a contradiction. Thus
\[
 \min\{\mu_{\varnothing},W(\Pi)\}
 =\min\{\mu_{\varnothing},V(\Pi)\}.
\]
Since \(\mathcal E_\Pi\subseteq\mathcal U(\Pi)\), we have \(V(\Pi)\le W(\Pi)\). If \(W(\Pi)\le\mu_{\varnothing}\), the displayed identity forces \(V(\Pi)=W(\Pi)\). Without such a condition, equality need not hold for the nonempty families.

For example, take independent standard normals \(U,U_Z\), with \(A=0.1U\), \(Z=U_Z\), \(Y=A^2\), manipulable variables \(\{A,Z\}\), and domains \(D_A=[1,2]\), \(D_Z=[-1,1]\). The only graph edge is \(A\to Y\), so the only nonempty MIS is \(\{A\}\), with value \(1\). Intervening on \(Z\) alone gives \(\E[A^2]=0.01\). At singleton resolution, therefore, \(V=0.01<W=1\); the removed action reduces to the null intervention. Observation-only rounds do not make the null action eligible in the nonempty recommendation protocol.

For \(X_1\to X_2\to Y\), intervening on both variables gives \(X'=\{X_2\}\); fixing \(X_2\) already blocks the effect of \(X_1\).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:price}]
Every union of clusters in \(\Pi\) is a union of clusters in a refinement \(\Pi'\), and every such union is available at the singleton partition. The population minima are therefore over nested action families:
\[
 V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})\le V(\Pi')\le V(\Pi).
\]
Equality between the first and last terms holds exactly when a fine-optimal intervention belongs to \(\mathcal U(\Pi)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}]
For part (i), use \textsc{ParallelParent} with manipulable variables \(\{X_1,X_2\}\), domains \([-3,3]^2\), edges \(X_1\to Y\) and \(X_2\to Y\), latent \(X_1\leftrightarrow X_2\), and partition \(\{\{X_1,X_2\},\{Y\}\}\). Choose the objective curvature so that the best \(\{X_2\}\)-only intervention has value \(\beta\), while the joint arm attains \(y^\star=0\). Deleting \(X_1\to Y\) preserves the quotient through \(X_2\to Y\) but leaves \(\operatorname{MIS}(\widehat{\Gg})=\{\{X_2\}\}\). Every executed intervention and eligible incumbent therefore has regret at least \(\beta\), giving \(R_T\ge\beta T\). The whole-cluster arm remains globally optimal, so \(\Delta(\Pi)=0\).

For part (ii), Proposition~\ref{prop:contract} makes the trajectory and regret constant over \(E_\Pi(\Gg)\). Adding and subtracting \(V(\Pi)\) gives
\[
r_t=\Delta(\Pi)+\mu(\hat z_t)-V(\Pi).
\]
The second term is nonnegative because every incumbent is an executed cluster-union action. Summing over \(t\) gives the stated decomposition.
 \end{proof}

\subsection{Lossless coarsening}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-price}]
Fix a nonempty \(X\subseteq\mathbf X\) and \(x\in D(X)\).
Write \(B=X^\Pi\setminus X\) and
\[
D_B(x):=\{b:(x,b)\in D(X^\Pi)\}.
\]
If \(B=\emptyset\), the intervention is already complete.

Otherwise, let \(\mathbf U_B\) be the exogenous ancestors of \(B\)
after intervening on \(X\), and let \(\mathbf U_Y\) be the
exogenous ancestors of \(Y\) after intervening on \(X^\Pi\).
Recursive substitution in the SCM gives measurable functions
\(g_x,h_x\) such that the natural response of \(B\) is
\[
B(x)=g_x(\mathbf U_B),
\]
and the target under the completed intervention \((X=x,B=b)\) is
\[
h_x(b,\mathbf U_Y).
\]
These representations use the same disturbances and functions for
all completion values \(b\). Under the original intervention
\(\doo(X=x)\), the target is therefore
\[
h_x\bigl(g_x(\mathbf U_B),\mathbf U_Y\bigr).
\]

Exogenous separation makes \(\mathbf U_B\) and \(\mathbf U_Y\)
disjoint collections of mutually independent exogenous variables.
Hence \(B(x)\) is independent of \(\mathbf U_Y\).
Integrating over these independent disturbances gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
f_X(x)
&=\int \E\!\left[h_x(b,\mathbf U_Y)\right]\,
        \mathrm dP_{B(x)}(b)\\
&=\int f_{X^\Pi}(x,b)\,\mathrm dP_{B(x)}(b)\\
&\ge \inf_{b\in D_B(x)} f_{X^\Pi}(x,b)\\
&\ge \mu^\star(X^\Pi).
\end{aligned}
\]
Full intervention support justifies restricting the integral to
admissible completion values. Taking the minimum over \(x\) yields
\[
\mu^\star(X^\Pi)\le\mu^\star(X).
\]
Thus completing a fine-optimal intervention gives a cluster-union
intervention with no larger population value. The reverse inequality
follows because every cluster-union intervention is also a fine
intervention. Therefore \(V(\Pi)=V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})\).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why exogenous separation matters.}
Let \(U_1,U_2\) be independent and uniform on \([-1,1]\), and consider
\[
X_1=U_1,\qquad
X_2=U_2,\qquad
Y=X_1^2+(X_2-U_2)^2.
\]
Group \(X_1,X_2\) into one manipulable cluster, with intervention
domain \([-1,1]^2\). Full intervention support holds.
Under \(\doo(X_1=0)\), the natural response \(X_2=U_2\) cancels
the disturbance in the target equation, giving \(Y=0\).
Under a whole-cluster intervention,
\[
f_{\{X_1,X_2\}}(x_1,x_2)
=x_1^2+x_2^2+\tfrac13.
\]
Consequently,
\[
V(\Pi_{\mathrm{fine}})=0,\qquad
V(\Pi)=\tfrac13,\qquad
\Delta(\Pi)=\tfrac13.
\]
Separation fails because \(U_2\) determines the added variable
\(X_2\) and still affects \(Y\) after both variables are fixed.
Fixing \(X_2\) removes its natural dependence on \(U_2\), which is
precisely what made the fine intervention effective.

\subsection{Refinement results}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:recovery}]
Because \(\mathcal F\) contains a fine-optimal intervention,
\[
\min_{X\in\mathcal F}\mu^\star(X)=y^\star.
\]
Incumbent consistency therefore gives
\(\mu(\hat z_t)\to y^\star\) almost surely, and hence \(r_t\to0\)
almost surely. Applying the Cesàro mean theorem on each convergent
sample path gives
\[
\frac{R_T}{T}
=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t\longrightarrow0.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:adaptive-invariance}]
Before the first split, every graph-dependent operation factors through $(\Pi_0,\Gg^{\Pi_0})$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:contract}. The split time, the split clusters and the new arms are functions of the trajectory prefix and of $\mathcal H$ alone, and the new arms carry graph-free priors. Induction over trials therefore gives equal trajectories.
\end{proof}

\section{Acquisition and numerical estimation}
\label{app:estimators}

\subsection{Acquisition rules}

After global trial $t$, let $\mu_{X,t}(x)$ and $\sigma_{X,t}(x)$ be the posterior mean and standard deviation of arm $X$, conditioned on its available interventional data, and let $y_t^{\mathrm{best}}$ be the best observed interventional outcome. For minimization, expected improvement is
\[
 \operatorname{EI}_{X,t}(x)
 :=\E\!\left[
 \bigl(y_t^{\mathrm{best}}-f_X(x)\bigr)_+
 \mid\mathcal D_t\right].
\]
The arm-based implementation selects an arm and value maximizing $\operatorname{EI}_{X,t}(x)/c(X,x)$, where $c(X,x)=|X|$ in the controlled experiments. Under noisy feedback this is a plug-in criterion using the smallest measured outcome; unaffordable arms are masked before purchase. MCBO instead uses its optimistic mechanism acquisition, with multiplier $\beta=10$ in $\mu+\beta\sigma\eta$, not $\sqrt{10}$. The executed acquisition is \texttt{qSimpleRegret} with $q=1$ and 128 Sobol samples. Residual draws are fixed within each acquisition optimization and broadcast across candidate batches.

Population scoring does not select the recommendation. Static minimization recommends the purchased point with the smallest measured outcome, breaking ties by canonical arm order and then event index. MCBO maximization recommends the eligible point with the largest measured outcome, breaking ties by first occurrence. The population value of this data-selected point need not improve monotonically. Population-best-visited values are separate oracle diagnostics; algorithm, measurement and scoring random streams are separately keyed.


\subsection{Causal-effect estimation}

The complete identification check is performed on the fine ADMG for CBO or the supplied C-DAG for QCBO. When a query is identified and its functional is supported, the functional is instantiated using the corresponding plug-in estimator; otherwise, the arm uses the observational fallback.

For a valid backdoor adjustment set \(Z\), we estimate the causal effect by
\[
\widehat{\E}[Y\mid\doo(X=x)]
=
\int
\widehat{\E}[Y\mid X=x,Z=z]\,
\mathrm d\widehat P(z).
\]
 
Complete identification determines whether a causal effect is a functional of the observational distribution, whereas backdoor, frontdoor, and $g$-computation describe the functionals currently implemented.

In the corrected static protocol, arm GPs learn a Gaussian likelihood variance on the raw target scale, initialized at $\max(0.01,0.1\operatorname{Var}(Y))$ and bounded by $[10^{-6},10^6]$; rebuilt models are fitted before acquisition evaluation. Exact population expectations are reserved for post-run scoring.

For coherent dynamic multivariate nonroot mechanisms, an intrinsic-coregionalization GP supplies the mean and latent task covariance. Joint-row leave-one-out residuals hold out all outputs of one observational row together: with $\alpha=K^{-1}y$ and output indices $I$, the residual is $([K^{-1}]_{II})^{-1}\alpha_I$. Their centered sample covariance $S$ is replaced by $0.9S+0.1\operatorname{diag}(S)+10^{-8}\max\{\operatorname{tr}(S)/m,1\}I_m$ and added to latent predictive covariance, excluding the stock likelihood to avoid double counting. This is a residual approximation, not recovery of pure structural noise. Multivariate roots use their joint empirical law. Integration retains within-input task covariance, not a globally correlated posterior function across particles.


\section{Experimental details}
\label{app:additional-results}
\paragraph{Controlled suite.} Historical exact-response runs are archived separately. The present comparison uses noisy measurements and data-selected recommendations; its result floats remain marked pending until the complete frozen matrix passes its audit. Table~\ref{tab:minimal-taxonomy} specifies the graph perturbations.

The corrected protocol uses 100 shared observational rows and three arm-keyed initial measurements per arm, with one noisy SCM row per purchase. Independent standard Gaussian disturbances, including shared latent disturbances, follow the declared SCM. All initial, sequential and refinement data cost one unit per intervened variable, within budgets $B=100$ for ParallelParent/FrontDoor and $B=120$ for MediatedChain. Domains are $[-3,3]$ for manipulators except MediatedChain's $X_2\in[-2,2]$; natural mediator responses are not clipped. Population scoring evaluates the measured-data recommendation after purchase. Endpoints are its regret at $B$ and the sum of regret at integer costs $12,\ldots,B$, carrying forward the latest recommendation. The frozen matrix has 24 configurations and 30 paired seeds (2000--2029); complete audited corrected results are reported separately from the historical runs.

The committed-graph CEO comparator receives the same data and budget, with one observable candidate DAG, learned Gaussian likelihood and 35 acquisition anchors. Its DAG cannot represent the latent confounding in ParallelParent or FrontDoor. The upstream ``noiseless'' callback replays the purchased noisy row, without another measurement or oracle query. The adapter masks unaffordable arms and acquires no additional observational rows; this is not a test of graph-uncertainty learning.

\paragraph{Family suite.} Historical CBO-family ToyGraph, CompleteGraph (``Synthetic'') and SimplifiedCoralGraph runs are archived separately. The completed dynamic suite and the ongoing corrected MCBO suite use the protocols below. Historical nonstationary dynamic values and population-best-visited MCBO scores are not reused.

The corrected MCBO matrix compares fine/full-menu, fine/coarse-menu and quotient/coarse-menu configurations on both SCMs, with 20 paired seeds (2000--2019) and 100 sequential rounds. Initialization supplies five observational rows and two rows per nonnull allowed target, keyed by target. ToyGraph at zero additive noise is scored exactly; PSA reward is negative PSA and uses an independent 100,000-draw scoring stream. Toy's null action is eligible when in its menu, whereas PSA observational rows are not eligible recommendations. The primary paired contrast holds the coarse menu fixed; the full-menu comparison is separate. Final recommendation reward and its sum across rounds are reported without an equal-cost claim. An additional 80 edited runs test protected complete-trace equality: delete $X\to Z$ in ToyGraph and add aspirin$\to$statin in PSAGraph. All 120 main runs and the corresponding protected pairs are required for their respective analyses.

The coherent dynamic protocol compares the same three menu/model configurations on stationary, independent and \texttt{nonstat\_regularized} SCMs, using 20 paired seeds, 100 observational trajectories, three slices, ten scheduler trials per slice and 100 acquisition anchors. Trial zero is observational bookkeeping, giving nine purchases per slice. The new regularized companion replaces the change-point denominator by $\sqrt{1+X_0^2}$ at $t=1$, so $Z_1=-X_1/\sqrt{1+X_0^2}+Z_0+\varepsilon_{Z,1}$; its graph includes $X_0\to Z_1$ and $Z_1\to Y_2$. The original reciprocal-normal objective is excluded from primary population comparisons. Predictive integration uses 1,024 particles, checked separately against 4,096; learner feedback uses 2,048 common-noise particles and post-run scoring uses 100,000 independent particles. Each slice is scored under its committed intervention prefix. These are fixed-round finite-Monte-Carlo population comparisons, not single-row measurements, equal-cost comparisons or regret against a known global optimum.

Monte Carlo scoring uncertainty is reported separately. Frozen source, runtime and event checks reject incomplete matrices; engineering pilots are not replication evidence.
